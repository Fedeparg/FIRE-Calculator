# Derivatives: what they are, how risky they are and how they are taxed

A **derivative** is a contract whose value **depends on another asset** (the
*underlying*): a stock, an index, a commodity, a currency… You do not buy the
company or the index; you buy a contract that gains or loses as the underlying
moves. They are used to **hedge** a risk or to **speculate**, and almost always with
**leverage**.

## The most common ones

- **Futures**: an agreement to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price on a future
  date. They are settled daily by differences.
- **Options**: give the **right** (not the obligation) to buy (*call*) or sell
  (*put*) at a given price and date. The buyer pays a **premium** that can be lost
  entirely.
- **Warrants**: options issued by a financial institution and traded on a market
  as if they were a security.
- **Knock-outs and turbos**: leveraged products that are **cancelled** if the
  underlying touches a level (the *barrier*); when that happens, what you invested
  is lost (or nearly).
- **CFDs** (contracts for difference): you receive or pay the difference in the
  underlying's price between opening and closing, without ever owning it.

## Leverage: winning and losing faster

**Leverage** lets you control a position larger than the money you put in. At 10×,
a 5% rise in the underlying gives 50% on what you invested... and a 5% fall gives
−50%. With a knock-out barrier, a moderate fall is enough to lose **100%** of what
you invested. With some products (futures, CFDs, written options) you can even lose
**more** than you put in.

That is why they are products for people who understand exactly what they are
buying: they are not a "faster" alternative to an index fund, but a different kind
of risk. See also [diversification and risk](/aprende/diversificacion-y-riesgo).

## How they are taxed in Spain

In general terms, the result of buying and selling derivatives is a **capital gain
or loss** that goes into the [savings
base](/aprende/fiscalidad-del-ahorro): the year's gains and losses are added up, can
offset each other, and the balance is taxed at the savings brackets (19% to 30%). Net
losses are carried forward for four years, as the guide on [capital gains when you
sell](/aprende/plusvalias-al-vender) explains. Some specific products (some settled
as interest, or held for hedging) have their own treatment, so check each one.

**The two-month rule.** The rule that blocks a loss if you buy back the same
security within two months before or after is written for **securities and
participations**. The doctrine of the tax authority's General Directorate (for
example, binding rulings V2172-21 and V3282-18) holds that **futures, options and
CFDs** are derivative financial instruments, not negotiable securities, so that rule
is **not** applied to them in the same way as to shares or ETFs. **Warrants, turbos
and certificates** trade as securities, and there the answer depends on the specific
product and on whether there are homogeneous securities: do not assume either way,
and ask.

Derivatives with a foreign underlying or broker can also bring reporting obligations
(for example, declaring assets and rights abroad). Check your case before filing your
return.

## Why Sextante does not track their price

Sextante records your derivatives (for example, those you import from Trade
Republic) with their buys and sells, and they **count in the capital gains report**.
But it **does not track their price**: a knock-out or a warrant has its own
identifier, expires, gets cancelled, and its quote depends on issuer, barrier and
maturity, something the price sources we use do not cover reliably. A missing or
wrong quote would distort your returns, so in the portfolio they sit in a separate,
collapsed section and **do not add to the totals** or the market value.
